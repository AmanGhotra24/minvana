The Great Molasses Flood of 1919: When Boston Drowned in Syrup

A 50-foot steel tank held 2.3 million gallons of molasses over a Boston street. On an unusually warm January day, the rivets gave out — and a 25-foot wave moved at 35 miles an hour through the North End.

60 SEC Your 60 Seconds

  • On January 15, 1919, at about 12:30 pm, a riveted steel tank — 50 feet tall, 90 feet across — burst at 529 Commercial Street in Boston's North End. It held 2.3 million gallons of molasses, weighing roughly 13,000 tons.
  • The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company. It had been filled days earlier with molasses that kept fermenting, building pressure — and January 15 was an unusually warm winter day, around 40°F, after a cold snap.
  • The wave was 25 feet high and moved at an estimated 35 miles per hour. It knocked a firehouse off its foundation, bent the elevated railway tracks, threw a five-ton truck into a building, and killed horses where they stood.
  • Harvard researchers later showed the warm molasses hit freezing January air and thickened within seconds — victims were trapped where they fell, and rescuers struggled to pull anyone free. 21 people died; about 150 were injured.
  • Cleanup took hundreds of men several weeks, using salt water and sand; Boston Harbor ran brown for months. In 1925 the company was found liable — a landmark corporate-negligence ruling, with settlements over $600,000. Locals said the streets smelled of molasses on hot days for decades.

0:00 The tank

The Purity Distilling Company stored molasses at 529 Commercial Street to make industrial alcohol — demand was high during World War One. The steel tank, built in 1915, was enormous: 50 feet tall and 90 feet in diameter, holding 2.3 million gallons. Residents had heard it groaning and leaking for years; the company painted it brown to hide the seepage. In January 1919 it was filled with a fresh load of molasses that continued to ferment, releasing gas and building pressure inside the sealed tank.

0:20 The burst

January 15, 1919 was freakishly warm — about 40°F after days of cold. Around 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard what sounded like machine-gun fire: the rivets popping. Then the tank split open and 13,000 tons of molasses surged out as a wave witnesses described as 25 feet high, moving at roughly 35 miles per hour down Commercial Street. It flattened everything in its path — a firehouse lifted off its foundation, elevated train girders twisted, a five-ton truck hurled into a building, an elevated platform collapsed.

0:40 Why no one could escape

The cruelest detail came decades later, when Harvard graduate student Nicole Sharp modeled the flood for the American Physical Society. The molasses left the tank warm and relatively fluid — but it hit freezing January air and thickened within seconds. People caught in it were glued where they stood; rescuers wading in found they couldn't pull victims free, and some rescuers got stuck themselves. Outrunning a 35-mile-an-hour wave was never an option. Twenty-one people died and around 150 were injured, many scalded or crushed.

1:00 The aftermath

Hundreds of men worked for weeks to clean the North End, flushing streets with salt water and spreading sand for traction; Boston Harbor ran brown for months, and the cleanup crews tracked molasses across the city. The company blamed anarchists and a bomb; investigators found a poorly built tank instead. In 1925, after one of the longest civil trials in Massachusetts history, the company was found liable — one of the first landmark corporate-negligence rulings in America, with payouts exceeding $600,000. And for decades afterward, North End residents swore that on hot summer days, you could still smell molasses in the streets.
