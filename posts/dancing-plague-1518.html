The Dancing Plague of 1518: The City That Danced to Death

In July 1518, Strasbourg was seized by a dancing epidemic. Hundreds danced for weeks without rest — and some never stopped. Five hundred years later, nobody knows why.

60 SEC Your 60 Seconds

  • It began with one woman: Frau Troffea stepped into a Strasbourg street in July 1518 and started dancing. She couldn't stop. Within a week, about 34 people had joined her; within a month, roughly 400.
  • The dancers kept going day and night, many collapsing from exhaustion. Later accounts claim around 15 deaths a day at the peak — though the city's own records from 1518 don't confirm the death toll.
  • The city council's response made it worse: on doctors' advice, they hired musicians and built a stage, believing the afflicted needed to "dance it out."
  • The theories: ergot poisoning from moldy rye, mass psychogenic illness under famine and plague, or the feared "curse of St. Vitus." Historians still argue.
  • After about two months, it stopped as mysteriously as it began — one of history's strangest unsolved epidemics.

0:00 One woman starts dancing

In the searing July heat of 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into a street in Strasbourg — then a free city of the Holy Roman Empire — and began to dance. There was no music, no festival, no celebration. She danced until she collapsed from exhaustion, rested briefly, and then started again. She couldn't stop.

0:20 The city joins in

Within days, dozens of townspeople had joined her, seized by the same compulsion. Within a week, about 34 were dancing; within a month, roughly 400. The streets filled with men and women who hadn't slept or eaten in days — still moving. Chroniclers described them screaming and begging for help, unable to stop their own bodies.

0:40 The worst decision in public health history

The city council panicked. Consulting physicians, they concluded the dancing was caused by "hot blood" — and prescribed more dancing. They hired musicians and built a wooden stage so the afflicted could dance it out. Professional dancers were even brought in. It made everything worse: the music drew crowds, and the crowds fed the contagion. Only later did the council reverse course — banning public dancing and sending sufferers to pray at the shrine of St. Vitus in Saverne.

1:00 Why — and the mystery

Five centuries later, the cause is still debated. Some investigators blame ergot, a fungus on damp rye that causes convulsions and hallucinations — though historian John Waller has argued the ergot theory doesn't fit the evidence. Waller's own conclusion: mass psychogenic illness. Strasbourg in 1518 was crushed by famine, plague, and syphilis; people were terrified, superstitious, and convinced that St. Vitus could curse sinners with uncontrollable dancing. Under that pressure, the mind can move the body — and bodies followed. By September, the dancing stopped as suddenly as it had begun. The deadliest detail may be the least certain: the famous "15 deaths a day" figure comes from later retellings, not the city's contemporary records. What the records do agree on is stranger than any number: an entire city, dancing, and nobody could make it stop.
