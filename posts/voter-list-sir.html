13 Crore Names Missing from Voter Lists: Cleanup or Conspiracy?

An investigation found over 13 crore names struck from draft rolls across 30 states — and revealed a split inside the Election Commission itself.

Watch the 60-second version on YouTube

India is in the middle of a Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of its electoral rolls — a nationwide exercise to clean up voter lists. And it has become one of the country's biggest political controversies.

The 13-crore figure

An investigation by The Indian Express reported that the national SIR rollout had struck more than 13 crore names from draft voter rolls across 30 states and union territories. The opposition calls it "vote chori" — the systematic deletion of legitimate voters. The Election Commission calls it routine hygiene: removing the dead, duplicates, and people who have permanently shifted.

A split inside the Commission

The investigation's most explosive finding wasn't the number — it was what was happening inside the poll body. Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi reportedly objected on record at least 14 times over ten months to decisions on voter deletions, additions, appeals, and access to the digital databases behind the rolls. The Commission has disputed the characterization of an internal split and says its decisions were unanimous.

West Bengal: the flashpoint

The controversy is sharpest in West Bengal, where the ECI told the Supreme Court that 27.16 lakh voters were deleted during SIR, while 38.20 lakh appeals were filed against deletions and inclusions. As of September 18, only about 1.02 lakh of those appeals had been disposed of — leaving lakhs in limbo.

What the Court has said

In May 2026, the Supreme Court upheld the ECI's power to conduct the SIR under Article 324 of the Constitution — the exercise itself is legal. But legality of the power and fairness of the process are two different questions, and the Court continues to hear challenges on how deletions and appeals are being handled.

The question that actually matters

Here's the nuance most shouting matches skip: these are draft rolls, not final ones. A claims-and-objections process exists precisely so wrongly excluded voters can get back on the list. Nobody serious disputes that rolls should be cleaned — dead voters shouldn't vote. The real questions are about scale and due process: how many of 13 crore exclusions are genuine corrections, how many are eligible citizens failed by the verification machinery, and whether the appeals system can handle them in time.

Cleanup or conspiracy? The honest answer is that the data to settle it — transparent, auditable, booth-level numbers — is exactly what's being fought over.
