The "Midterm Curse"

October 2026 — United States

Your 60 seconds

Midterms began in 1790 as a built-in constitutional check on presidential power. The President's party historically loses congressional seats in almost every midterm cycle — the only exceptions: 1934, 1998, 2002. When the ruling party loses its majority, the country enters "divided government": the President's agenda can hit a wall — laws stall, judicial confirmations tighten, and the opposition gains subpoena power.

0:00 — The hook

Every two years, the sitting U.S. President faces a political wrecking ball: the Midterm Elections. For over two centuries, they have almost always punished the party in power. Since the Civil War, the president's party has lost ground in the House in 38 of 41 midterms — with only three exceptions: 1934, 1998, and 2002.

0:20 — The context

It started in 1790, right in the middle of George Washington's first term — the first midterm election cycle in American history. The Constitution set House terms at just two years (Article I, Section 2), turning every mid-presidency into a national report card. Historically, voters use that report card to check the President's power, stripping the ruling party of seats in Congress almost every single cycle.

0:40 — The twist

When the ruling party loses its majority, the country enters "divided government." Overnight, the President's agenda can hit a wall: laws stall, judicial confirmations tighten — the Senate's advice-and-consent role covers cabinet picks and federal judges, including Supreme Court nominees — and the opposition gains subpoena power under House Rule XI, allowing it to launch investigations into the administration.

1:00 — The takeaway

  • Midterms began in 1790 as a built-in constitutional check on presidential power.
  • The President's party historically loses congressional seats in almost every midterm cycle — the only exceptions: 1934, 1998, 2002.
