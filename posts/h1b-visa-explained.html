H-1B Visa Explained: Is It Good or Bad for America?

The CEOs of Google and Microsoft both arrived on it. Critics call it a job-killer; researchers say every visa creates 1.83 American jobs. Here is how the H-1B actually works — and what the numbers say.

60 SEC Your 60 Seconds

  • The H-1B is America's skilled work visa for engineers, scientists, and doctors. You cannot apply for it yourself — a US employer must sponsor you, prove the role needs specialized skills, and pay at least the prevailing wage.
  • Over 700,000 H-1B workers are in the US today — still under 0.5% of all American jobs — and more than 70% are Indian nationals. Congress caps new visas at 85,000 a year (65,000 general + 20,000 for US master's graduates).
  • In 2026, about 470,000 people registered for those 85,000 spots — a lottery. Since February 2026 the lottery is weighted by salary: higher-paid roles get up to four entries instead of one.
  • A visa lasts 3 years, extendable to 6. A green card is a separate, much longer queue — Indians face a 10–15 year wait.
  • The benefits case: researchers estimate every H-1B creates 1.83 jobs for US-born workers, and holders pay tens of billions in taxes. The criticism: outsourcing firms gamed the system to cut labor costs — which is why a $100,000 fee on new petitions was proposed in 2025, then blocked by federal courts in 2026.

0:00 What it is

The H-1B is a temporary US work visa for "specialty occupations" — jobs that require at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. In practice that means software engineers, data scientists, researchers, and doctors. It was created by the Immigration Act of 1990, when American companies argued they could not find enough STEM graduates at home.

The defining feature: it is entirely employer-driven. No individual can self-petition. A company registers you in the annual lottery, and if you are selected, files a petition with US Citizenship and Immigration Services — including a Labor Condition Application certifying it will pay you the prevailing wage for your role and city, so the hire does not undercut local workers.

0:20 How the lottery works now

Demand crushes supply every year. For the 2026 cycle, roughly 470,000 registrations chased 85,000 visas — selection odds near one in five. Registration costs $215 and happens each March; petitions for winners are filed from April, and work can start October 1.

The big 2026 change: the lottery is no longer random. Since February 2026, entries are weighted by the Department of Labor wage level for the job — a top-tier salary gets four entries in the pool, an entry-level one gets a single entry. The first run under the new rule saw registrations fall nearly 40%, and over 70% of selected beneficiaries held a US advanced degree.

0:40 The good-or-bad debate

The case for the program rests on two numbers. A widely cited study by the American Enterprise Institute and the Partnership for a New American Economy found that each H-1B visa creates 1.83 jobs for US-born workers over seven years — the logic being that scarce specialists let companies expand, which creates surrounding roles in sales, marketing, and operations. Holders are also high earners who pay substantial federal, state, and payroll taxes. And the cultural proof points are famous: Satya Nadella and Sundar Pichai both began their American careers on H-1Bs before running Microsoft and Google.

The case against it: staffing and outsourcing firms flooded the lottery with applications for lower-paid roles, which critics say suppressed wages and, in some cases, saw American workers train their foreign replacements. That backlash produced the most dramatic policy fight in the program's history — a September 2025 presidential proclamation imposing a $100,000 fee on new H-1B petitions. Federal courts struck the fee down in June 2026, ruling it an unauthorized tax; the government appealed, extended the proclamation through September 2027, and is currently not collecting the fee while the litigation plays out.

1:00 The takeaway

Strip away the noise and the H-1B is a rationing system for global talent: 85,000 golden tickets a year, allocated by salary-weighted lottery, feeding an American tech economy that still cannot produce enough engineers domestically. Whether that trade is good for America depends on which number you trust — 1.83 jobs created, or the wage gap critics point to. The courts, Congress, and the next lottery will keep arguing it out. The one certainty: with nearly half a million applicants a year, the line is not getting shorter.
