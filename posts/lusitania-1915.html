One Torpedo, Two Explosions: The Sinking of the Lusitania

May 7, 1915 — off the Old Head of Kinsale, Ireland

Your 60 seconds

On May 7, 1915, the German U-boat U-20 fired a single torpedo into the British liner Lusitania. A second, far larger explosion followed — its cause still disputed. The ship sank in 18 minutes; 1,198 of the 1,959 aboard died, including 128 Americans. Her manifest listed 4,200 cases of rifle cartridges and 1,250 crates of shrapnel shells. The British inquiry sat partly behind closed doors. The sinking turned American opinion against Germany; the US entered the war in April 1917.

0:00 — The warning

Before she sailed, the Imperial German Embassy placed newspaper notices warning travelers that the waters around Britain were a war zone and any ship entered them "at their own risk." 1,959 people boarded anyway.

0:20 — One torpedo, then a second blast

At about 2:10 PM, Kapitänleutnant Walther Schwieger's U-20 fired one torpedo from roughly 700 meters. It struck the starboard side — and then came a second, bigger explosion. What caused it? Historians still argue: ignited coal dust (oceanographer Roger Ballard's conclusion after months of study in 1993), a boiler explosion (suggested by 2012 tests at a US government research facility), disturbed aluminum powder, or the ship's own cargo. What experts largely rule out: the munitions themselves — small-arms ammunition is non-explosive in bulk, the shells were reportedly empty, and the fuses non-explosive.

0:40 — The secret cargo and the sealed inquiry

The two-page manifest filed with US Customs listed 4,200 cases of Remington rifle cartridges, 1,250 crates of shrapnel shells, and thousands of percussion fuses. Cunard openly admitted the cartridges days later; Britain long denied anything more. The Mersey inquiry into the sinking heard some evidence in secret. Decades of wreck dives — including Greg Bemis's expeditions in 1982, 2008, and 2011 — confirmed millions of rounds in the hold but never settled the second-blast question.

Why it matters

The Lusitania didn't single-handedly bring America into World War I — that took two more years and Germany's return to unrestricted submarine warfare. But the sinking, and the argument over what she secretly carried, turned American public opinion and gave the war's moral debate its defining image: a passenger liner, 18 minutes, 1,198 dead.
