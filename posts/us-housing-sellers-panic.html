When Sellers Panic

Half of Dallas just cut their price. Here's what happens next.

Your 60 seconds

  • 1 in 3 listings in Salt Lake City, Denver and Portland took a price cut in September — the highest share in years. In San Jose, the median cut was $77,000.
  • Sellers are cutting faster than last year (34 days to the first cut) but smaller — 5%, the smallest on record.
  • The cuts aren't bringing buyers back: pending sales are still down 4%.
  • 2008? Homeowners hold $36 trillion in equity. 1% are underwater vs 23% in 2010. Foreclosures: 1 in 632 homes, not 1 in 45.
  • A crash needs forced sellers, no equity, and a flood of supply. None of the three are here.

The cascade

In September 2026, price cuts hit their highest share in years across the country. Salt Lake City led at 33.6% of listings, followed by Denver (32.1%), Portland (31.6%), Indianapolis (30.5%) and Phoenix (29.0%), per Realtor.com's analysis of listings as of September 30. The dollar amounts were biggest where prices are highest: the median cut in San Jose was nearly $77,000 off a ~$1.2 million median list price.

Realtor.com's research shows sellers are reacting quicker than in 2025 — a median of 34 days to the first cut — but cutting less: a cumulative 5% of the initial asking price, the smallest concession on record. Only about 5.6% of homes were delisted in September, in line with last year. Sellers are staying in the game and adjusting, not retreating.

The risk they run is the "problem house" stigma: listings that sit too long make buyers ask what's wrong with the home. Instead of deeper cuts, many sellers are offering concessions — closing-cost assistance or mortgage rate buydowns.

Why this isn't 2008

The Federal Reserve's September 2026 Z.1 report puts total US home value at $49.8 trillion against $14 trillion in mortgage debt — leaving $35.8 trillion in homeowner equity, with 71.9% of homes owned free and clear. Only about 1.1% of mortgage holders are underwater, versus 23% in 2010. Foreclosure filings ran 367,460 in all of 2025, against 2.87 million in 2010 — roughly 1 in 632 homes now versus 1 in 45 then. Average credit scores sit above 750 versus ~680 before the crash, and subprime lending is effectively gone.

What I'm watching

Realtor.com economist Jake Krimmel frames the open question: whether deeper or repeated reductions can bring buyers back and get more homes under contract. Pending sales fell 4.1% year over year in September — the steepest drop since March 2025. Until that turns, the cuts keep coming. And the next question: what breaks first — sellers, or rates?
