The Tunguska Explosion (1908): 1,000 Hiroshimas, No Crater

Something detonated over Siberia with the force of a thousand atomic bombs. It flattened 80 million trees, made the night sky glow over Europe for days — and left no crater behind. Over a century later, the mystery is still open.

60 SEC Your 60 Seconds

  • On June 30, 1908, at 7:14 in the morning, an explosion estimated at 10–15 megatons — about a thousand Hiroshimas — tore through the sky above the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia. It was the largest impact event in recorded history.
  • Some 80 million trees were flattened across 830 square miles in a radial butterfly pattern. People were knocked off their feet 40 miles away; seismographs across Eurasia registered the blast. For days afterward, the night sky over Europe glowed bright enough to read a newspaper at midnight.
  • The first scientific expedition, led by Leonid Kulik, didn't reach the site until 1927 — 19 years later. Kulik expected a crater and meteorite fragments. He found no crater at all. The trees at the very center were still standing, stripped bare like telegraph poles — the signature of an airburst: the explosion happened in the sky, not on the ground.
  • The accepted explanation is a stony asteroid or comet fragment, 50–80 meters wide, that vaporized 5–10 kilometers above the surface. Wilder theories — a miniature black hole, an antimatter event, even Tesla's directed-energy weapon — have no supporting evidence whatsoever.
  • The sobering part: the object was too small to detect before arrival. Over a city, it kills millions. The Siberian forest absorbed what civilization could never have survived. June 30 is now the UN's International Asteroid Day.

0:00 One thousand Hiroshimas. No crater.

The morning of June 30, 1908, began like any other in the Siberian taiga — and then the sky exploded. Witnesses described a fireball as bright as the sun tearing across the sky, followed by a blast that flattened everything in its path. The energy released was on the order of 10 to 15 megatons of TNT. For scale: that's roughly a thousand times the bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

0:20 80 million trees, one glowing sky

The devastation was almost incomprehensible: some 80 million pine trees snapped and flattened across 2,150 square kilometers, radiating outward in a butterfly pattern from the epicenter. People 40 miles away were thrown off their feet. Seismographs across the entire Eurasian continent picked up the shockwave. And then came the strangest detail of all: for several nights afterward, the sky over Europe glowed so brightly that people could read newspapers outdoors at midnight.

0:40 The crater that wasn't there

Here's the twist. When Soviet scientist Leonid Kulik finally reached the blast zone in 1927 — nineteen years after the event — he expected to find a massive impact crater and chunks of meteorite. Instead he found no crater at all. Even weirder: the trees directly beneath the blast were still standing, stripped of every branch like telegraph poles. That pattern is the fingerprint of an airburst — a detonation that happened miles up in the atmosphere, never touching the ground.

1:00 Too small to see coming

The scientific consensus today: a stony asteroid or comet fragment, 50 to 80 meters across, disintegrated explosively at an altitude of 5 to 10 kilometers. An icy body would explain why no fragments were ever recovered — it simply vaporized. The fringe theories — a miniature black hole, an antimatter strike, Tesla's death ray — have no evidence behind them. What should keep you up at night is simpler: the rock was too small to spot before it arrived. Over a major city, the same event kills millions. The forest took the hit so civilization didn't have to. Every June 30th, the world marks International Asteroid Day in its honor.
