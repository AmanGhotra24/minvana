Hair Loss: What Actually Works (and What's a Waste of Money)

Nearly all male hair loss comes down to one hormone shrinking your follicles. Most of what the ads sell you can't touch it. Two treatments can — and there's a catch neither advertises.

0:00 What hair loss actually is

Most people talk about hair loss as if it's one condition with many cures. In reality, about 95% of hair loss in men is a single condition: androgenetic alopecia — male-pattern baldness. The mechanism is well understood. A hormone called dihydrotestosterone (DHT), converted from testosterone by an enzyme called 5-alpha reductase, binds to genetically susceptible hair follicles and progressively shrinks them — a process called miniaturization. Each growth cycle produces thinner, shorter hairs until the follicle stops producing visible hair at all.

Why the temples and crown? Those follicles carry more androgen receptors and are more sensitive to DHT. The follicles at the back and sides of the scalp are largely resistant — which is exactly why they're the ones used in transplants. Genetics set your susceptibility, and it comes from both parents' sides, not just your maternal grandfather (that's a myth).

0:15 What doesn't work (and why)

This mechanism explains why most of what the market sells fails. Specialty shampoos wash the scalp, not the follicles beneath it. Oils may condition existing hair but can't reverse DHT-driven miniaturization. Biotin only helps if you're actually biotin-deficient, which is rare in healthy adults. None of them address the hormone at the center of the condition.

That doesn't make the entire industry fraudulent — cosmetic products can make thinning hair look better. But "looks better" and "regrows hair" are different claims, and the evidence supports only the first one for most of these products.

0:25 What the evidence supports

Two treatments have the strongest clinical evidence behind them, and they work through different mechanisms:

Minoxidil (available over the counter as foam or liquid) was originally developed as a blood-pressure drug; hair growth was a side effect. Applied to the scalp, it prolongs the hair's growth phase and improves blood flow around follicles. It doesn't touch DHT — it essentially coaxes weakened follicles back into production.

Finasteride (prescription-only, 1 mg daily for hair loss) does the opposite: it blocks the 5-alpha reductase enzyme, lowering DHT levels in the scalp by roughly 60–70%. It tackles the cause. It requires a prescription precisely because it's a systemic hormonal intervention — and that comes with a real side-effect profile (including sexual side effects in a small percentage of users) that deserves a genuine conversation with a doctor before starting.

The combination is where the strongest data sits: a 12-month retrospective study of 502 men found that 92% were stable or improved on combined minoxidil-finasteride therapy. Two caveats: it was retrospective (observational, not a randomized trial), and "stable or improved" includes men who simply stopped losing — not all of them regrew hair.

0:45 Transplants and the catch nobody advertises

Hair transplants work by relocating DHT-resistant follicles from the back of the scalp to thinning areas. They don't create new hair — it's redistribution, and the supply of donor follicles is finite. They also don't stop the underlying process: without continued medical treatment, the native hair around the transplanted area can keep thinning.

And here's the central catch for medical treatment: these are maintenance therapies, not cures. Stop minoxidil or finasteride and, within months, the follicles return to their DHT-driven decline and the gains reverse. Anyone starting treatment should go in understanding it's a long-term commitment.

The bottom line

Hair loss is mostly a hormone problem, and the evidence backs exactly the treatments that address the mechanism: minoxidil to stimulate follicles, finasteride to block DHT, and the combination doing the most. Everything else is cosmetics or wishful thinking. If you're considering treatment, the decision is worth making with a doctor who can walk through the trade-offs honestly — because the one thing the evidence is unambiguous about is that nothing here is free, permanent, or one-and-done.
