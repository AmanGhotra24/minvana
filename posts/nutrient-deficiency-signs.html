6 Signs Your Body Needs More Nutrients: What the Research Actually Shows

Inspired by one of the most-watched health videos on YouTube — but we checked every claim against the evidence. Six body signals that genuinely hold up, two popular ones that don't, and why only a blood test gets the final word.

60 SEC Your 60 Seconds

  • Cracks at the corners of your mouth (angular cheilitis) are linked in studies to low B vitamins — especially B2 and B12 — plus iron. Lip balm can't fix a nutritional gap.
  • Bleeding gums when you brush are the classic first sign of scurvy — severe vitamin C deficiency. (Gum disease is the more common cause; context matters.)
  • Struggling to see at night? Night blindness is one of the earliest documented signs of vitamin A deficiency.
  • Can't stop crunching ice? It's called pagophagia — and studies found it in 16% to over half of iron-deficient patients, resolving after iron repletion.
  • Thinning outer eyebrows — the "Queen Anne's sign" — is a recognized clinical clue of a slowing thyroid, and the thyroid needs iodine to make its hormones.
  • Snapping at everyone? Irritability is listed among the earliest signs of thiamine (B1) deficiency, appearing within weeks.
  • The honest framing: every one of these signs has multiple causes. Signs point — only a blood test confirms. And two famous claims didn't survive fact-checking: magnesium for leg cramps (Cochrane: no meaningful benefit) and white nail spots meaning zinc deficiency (usually just minor trauma).

0:00 The payoff, up front

Your body is sending SOS signals. Dr. Eric Berg's video on deficiency signs has been watched over 15 million times — but viral doesn't mean verified. So we took six of his most striking claims and checked each against the actual research. Six held up. Here's what they are, and what they really mean.

0:06 Cracked mouth corners → B vitamins

Those painful splits at the corners of your mouth have a medical name: angular cheilitis. A review in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology highlights its association with deficiencies in B2 (riboflavin), B3, B6, and B12, as well as iron and zinc. Riboflavin deficiency is the textbook cause — the condition even has a dedicated name in nutrition literature, cheilosis.

The tell: the splits don't respond to lip balm. A topical fix can't correct a nutritional gap. But note the caveat — angular cheilitis also comes from fungal infection, dentures, and drooling, so it's a signal, not a diagnosis.

0:14 Bleeding gums → vitamin C

Bleeding gums when you brush are the classic first sign of scurvy — severe vitamin C deficiency. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements lists swollen, bleeding gums among scurvy's defining symptoms, alongside fatigue and impaired wound healing.

The honest framing: in developed countries, gum disease is a far more common cause of bleeding gums than scurvy. The sign is real; the most likely explanation is usually the ordinary one.

0:21 Night blindness → vitamin A

Struggling to see at night is one of the earliest documented signs of vitamin A deficiency — nyctalopia. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and the WHO both describe night blindness as the first step on the xerophthalmia spectrum, the eye disease of vitamin A deficiency that remains a leading cause of preventable blindness in children worldwide.

0:26 Ice craving → iron

Compulsive ice chewing has a clinical name — pagophagia — and its link to iron deficiency is one of the best-documented items on this list. A 2010 study in BMC Hematology of 262 outpatients found 45% reported pica, and 87% of that was ice chewing; the ice-chewers had lower ferritin and lower MCV. A French case-control study found 58% of iron-deficiency anemia patients had pica, 88% of it pagophagia. And the cravings typically resolve once iron is repleted.

The mechanism is still debated — one hypothesis is that chewing ice briefly sharpens alertness in the fatigued, foggy anemic brain. The association, though, is solid.

0:34 Thinning outer eyebrows → iodine / thyroid

Thinning or loss of the outer third of the eyebrows is a recognized clinical sign with a royal name: Queen Anne's sign (or Hertoghe's sign). Clinical reviews list it as a classic indicator of hypothyroidism — when thyroid hormone drops, hair follicles are pushed prematurely into their resting phase, and the outer eyebrow goes first.

The iodine connection: iodine is essential for manufacturing thyroid hormones, and the WHO notes iodine deficiency remains a global health issue affecting around two billion people. One nuance Berg skips — in developed countries, Hashimoto's thyroiditis is a more common cause of hypothyroidism than iodine deficiency. The sign points at the thyroid; the thyroid's story needs a lab test.

0:43 Irritability → vitamin B1

Snapping at everyone for no reason? The Cleveland Clinic lists irritability among thiamine (B1) deficiency symptoms, and reviews of the literature describe mood changes and irritability as among the earliest signs — appearing within weeks of depletion, alongside fatigue. Thiamine is central to converting food into energy for the brain; when it runs low, the nervous system complains first.

The caveat is obvious: irritability has a hundred causes. As an isolated sign it's weak; as part of a pattern — fatigue, poor appetite, tingling — it earns attention.

0:49 What didn't survive fact-checking

Two famous claims failed. Magnesium for leg cramps: a 2020 Cochrane systematic review (Garrison et al.) found magnesium unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention in older adults with idiopathic cramps, and found no trials at all for exercise-associated cramps. A 2023 American Family Physician evidence review concluded "probably not" effective for nocturnal leg cramps. White spots on nails meaning zinc deficiency: leukonychia is overwhelmingly caused by minor nail trauma, not zinc status — the evidence linking it to zinc is case-report level at best. Both were cut from the video.

The bottom line

Six of Berg's 23 signs hold up against the evidence — and they're genuinely useful things to know about your body. But every sign on this list is an association with multiple possible causes. The responsible reading: let the signs prompt a conversation and a blood test, not a supplement order. Signs point. Labs confirm.
